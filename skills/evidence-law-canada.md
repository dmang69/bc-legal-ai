# Evidence Law Canada

Legal information and drafting support only. Never assume evidence is admissible without testing purpose, source, form, and procedural context. Verify governing statutes, court rules, authorities, and pinpoints.

## Anchor authorities

- *R. v. Khan*, 1990 CanLII 77 (SCC)
- *R. v. Smith*, 1992 CanLII 79 (SCC)
- *R. v. Khelawon*, 2006 SCC 57
- *R. v. Bradshaw*, 2017 SCC 35
- *Ares v. Venner*, 1970 CanLII 5 (SCC)
- *White Burgess Langille Inman v. Abbott and Haliburton Co.*, 2015 SCC 23

Verify the forum-specific evidence legislation and procedural rules before use.

## Mandatory analytical sequence

For each item ask:
1. What proposition is it offered to prove?
2. Is that proposition material and is the item relevant?
3. Who created it, when, why, and from what knowledge?
4. Is it direct evidence, hearsay, lay opinion, expert opinion, or mixed?
5. Is it authenticated and complete?
6. Does an exclusionary rule or privilege apply?
7. Is there an exception or principled route to admission?
8. If admitted, what realistic weight does it have?
9. What rebuttal is likely?
10. What better evidence should be obtained?

## Relevance and record discipline

Every exhibit must have a one-sentence purpose. Distinguish core proof, corroboration, and background. Avoid bloated records that obscure material evidence.

## Hearsay

Hearsay is an out-of-court statement offered for its truth. First test purpose: notice, knowledge, state of mind, effect on listener, and sequence may be non-truth uses. If offered for truth, analyze necessity and threshold reliability; distinguish threshold reliability from ultimate weight. Identify each hearsay layer. State why direct evidence is unavailable/impractical, reliability markers, and precise purpose.

Red flags: delayed reconstructed memory, unknown author/time, cropped or edited screenshots, third-hand affidavit claims, and paraphrased witness statements.

## Business and contemporaneous records

Assess contemporaneity, knowledge or recording duty, ordinary-course practice, and absence of litigation motive. Regular records may still contain embedded hearsay. Examples include banking, payment, utility, inspection, maintenance, medical, and institutional records.

## Affidavits

Prefer personal knowledge: what the deponent saw, received, paid, photographed, attended, or said. Identify dates and sources and attach documents. Flag argument disguised as fact, speculative motive, legal conclusions, collective knowledge, and unsupported summaries. Information and belief must comply with the applicable rule and identify source and basis.

## Authentication and electronic evidence

Use native files, complete chains, account-control evidence, reply conduct, filenames, timestamps, metadata, and hash/integrity information where appropriate. Flag cropping, conversation gaps, uncertain account control, missing metadata, and unexplained time-zone/date integrity.

## Privilege

Screen solicitor-client, litigation, settlement, common-interest, derivative, and waiver issues before filing or exhibiting material. Relevance does not defeat privilege. Recommend sealing/redaction or counsel review where appropriate; never assume helpful privileged material can safely be disclosed.

## Opinion evidence

Lay witnesses generally describe perception rather than legal/technical conclusions. For experts test necessity, qualification, independence/impartiality, methodology, scope, and factual foundation. Apply the verified *White Burgess* framework and flag advocacy-shaped or selective opinions.

## Originals, copies, notice, and weight

Check completeness, signatures, legibility, alteration, and whether the content itself is disputed. Use judicial notice sparingly for notorious or readily verifiable matters, not contested social facts or specialized causal propositions.

Keep admissibility separate from weight. Weight factors include contemporaneity, neutrality, personal knowledge, consistency, completeness, motive, and ability to test by cross-examination.

## Tribunal versus court

Tribunals may receive broader material, but reliability, fairness, testability, and safe reliance remain material—especially on judicial review.

## Language bank

- "This paragraph contains hearsay offered for its truth without a demonstrated route to admission."
- "This exhibit is incomplete and has not been authenticated."
- "The statement is argumentative rather than factual."
- "The witness has not established personal knowledge of this proposition."
- "The statement is offered to prove notice, not the truth of its contents."
- "Any residual concern goes to weight rather than admissibility."

## Quality gate

Match every material proposition to a witness/exhibit; identify hearsay purpose and layers; use best available evidence; screen privilege; ensure complete, legible, dated, labelled exhibits; and distinguish FACT, ALLEGATION, LAW, ARGUMENT, INFERENCE, ASSUMPTION, and REMEDY.
