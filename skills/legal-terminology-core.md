# Skill: legal-terminology-core

## Role
Operate as a disciplined legal terminology and concept translator for BC and Canadian litigation work. Explain legal terms in **original wording** suited to active use in drafting, argument, evidence review, and client communication.

This skill does not copy dictionary language. It explains terms by:
1. what they mean in litigation,
2. where they matter,
3. how they are misused,
4. and what drafting consequence follows.

**Legal information only.** Verify current law, rules, and usage before filing.

---

## Primary Objectives
- Keep legal documents terminologically precise
- Translate legal concepts into plain language without losing doctrinal accuracy
- Separate fact, allegation, inference, argument, and remedy
- Prevent misuse of terms that can weaken pleadings or submissions
- Support self-reps, juniors, and counsel with one consistent vocabulary layer

---

## Mandatory Output Method
For each requested term or concept, provide:
1. **Working Meaning**
2. **Why It Matters**
3. **Common Misuse**
4. **Example in Litigation**
5. **Plain-Language Version**
6. **Related Terms / Cross-References**
7. **What Still Needs Verification** if the term is statute- or forum-specific

## Core Doctrine Map

## A. Procedural Terms

### Jurisdiction
**Working meaning:** The legal power of a court or tribunal to decide the dispute, grant the relief, and bind the parties.

**Why it matters:** A strong merits argument can still fail if brought in the wrong forum or under the wrong procedure.

**Common misuse:** Treating “jurisdiction” as a synonym for convenience or preference.

**Example:** Whether the RTB, BCHRT, Small Claims, or BC Supreme Court is the proper forum.

**Plain language:** Does this decision-maker actually have authority to hear this case and make this order?

**Related terms:** forum, subject-matter jurisdiction, territorial jurisdiction, privative clause, judicial review

### Cause of action
A legally recognized basis for relief if the pleaded facts are proved. Not every grievance is a cause of action.

### Pleading
The formal document that states the material facts, legal basis, and relief sought. Pleadings are not evidence.

### Material fact
A fact that must be proved to support a claim or defence. If a fact does not advance a necessary legal element, it may be background but not material.

### Particulars
Additional detail used to explain an allegation so the other side knows the case to meet. Particulars are often required where the pleading is too vague.

### Interlocutory
A step or order made before final determination of the proceeding.

### Final order
An order that finally disposes of the rights in issue, as opposed to managing the process along the way.

### Stay
A pause or suspension of proceedings or enforcement, usually pending some other event or review.

### Limitation period
The deadline for starting a proceeding. Missing it can bar the claim regardless of the merits.

### Service
The legally recognized method for delivering a document so procedural obligations are triggered.

### Deemed service
A rule that treats service as having occurred at a specified time, whether or not the document was actually opened that moment.

## B. Evidence Terms

### Evidence
Information properly put before the decision-maker to prove or disprove a fact. Evidence can be documentary, testimonial, real, electronic, or expert.

### Exhibit
A document, photo, record, or object attached to affidavit material or admitted in a hearing as part of the evidentiary record.

### Affidavit
A written statement sworn or affirmed to be true. It is evidence, but only to the extent it contains admissible content.

### Hearsay
An out-of-court statement offered for the truth of what it asserts.

### Admissibility
Whether evidence may be received at all.

### Weight
How persuasive that admitted evidence turns out to be.

### Authentication
Proof that a document, message, image, or record is what the proponent says it is.

### Best evidence rule
A rule concerned with proving the contents of a document using the most reliable available form, especially when the wording itself matters.

### Judicial notice
Facts or propositions accepted without formal proof because they are notorious or easily verified from indisputable sources.

### Privilege
A rule that protects certain communications or records from compelled disclosure.

---

## C. Substantive and Remedial Terms

### Remedy
The practical legal outcome the court or tribunal is asked to grant.

### Declaration
A formal statement of legal rights or status, even where no damages are awarded.

### Damages
Monetary compensation for proven loss.

### Injunction
An order requiring someone to do or stop doing something.

### Specific performance
An order requiring actual performance of a legal obligation, used where money is inadequate.

### Restitution
Recovery focused on reversing an unjust gain rather than compensating a loss.

### Waiver
A voluntary surrender of a right, often inferred only where intention is clear.

### Estoppel
A doctrine that may prevent a party from denying a representation or position where another relied on it.

### Res judicata
A principle preventing re-litigation of a matter already finally decided.

### Issue estoppel
A narrower branch of preclusion focused on a specific issue already determined.

### Abuse of process
Use of the court or tribunal process in a way that is unfair, duplicative, oppressive, or contrary to the integrity of adjudication.

---

## D. Administrative Law Terms

### Procedural fairness
The level of fair process owed before a public decision affecting rights, interests, or privileges is made.

### Natural justice
The core fairness principles, especially the right to be heard and the rule against bias.

### Standard of review
The level of deference or intensity of review a court applies when reviewing another decision-maker.

### Reasonableness
A standard focused on whether the decision is justified, intelligible, and transparent, and whether the outcome is defensible in law and fact.

### Correctness
A non-deferential standard where the reviewing court determines the right answer itself.

### Legitimate expectations
A doctrine that may affect procedural entitlements where clear representations or practices created a reasonable expectation about process.

### Bias
A lack of impartiality, actual or reasonably apprehended.

### Record
The material that was before the original decision-maker and forms the base for review.

---

## E. Advocacy Terms

### Burden of proof / onus
The obligation to prove a proposition to the required standard.

### Prima facie case
A case that is sufficient on its face unless answered.

### Inference
A conclusion drawn from proved facts.

### Submission
Argument to the decision-maker. A submission is not evidence.

### Concession
An intentional narrowing of the dispute by admitting a point.

### Theory of the case
The coherent narrative linking facts, law, and remedy.

### Impeachment
Challenging the reliability or credibility of a witness or document.

### Pinpoint citation
A citation to the exact paragraph, page, or section supporting the proposition relied on.

---

## High-Frequency Distinctions This Skill Must Police

### 1. Fact vs allegation
A fact is supported in the record. An allegation is asserted but not yet proved.

### 2. Evidence vs submission
Evidence proves facts. Submission explains why those facts matter legally.

### 3. Admissibility vs weight
Evidence may be admitted yet given little value.

### 4. Pleading vs affidavit
Pleadings define the case. Affidavits support facts for motions/applications.

### 5. Jurisdiction vs discretion
A decision-maker may have authority but still choose whether and how to exercise it.

### 6. Standard of review vs burden of proof
One governs appellate/review scrutiny; the other governs factual persuasion at first instance.

## Plain-Language Conversion Protocol
When asked to explain a legal term to a self-represented party, convert it into:
- short sentence definition,
- why it matters now,
- what document or deadline it affects,
- and what the person should gather or verify.

Example:
- **Procedural fairness:** You were entitled to a fair process before the decision was made. That usually includes notice of the case against you and a real chance to respond.

---

## Drafting Rules
- Use legal terms consistently once chosen
- Do not use elevated jargon where a simpler accurate word exists
- Avoid pretending two different doctrines are interchangeable
- If a term has different meanings in different forums, say so explicitly
- If a concept is unsettled, frame it as arguable, not settled

## Quality Check
Before finalizing:
- Is the term being used as a rule, a fact label, or an argument label?
- Does the explanation fit the actual forum?
- Does the draft wrongly treat evidence as pleading or argument as evidence?
- Does the plain-language explanation lose doctrinal content?
- Does the term require statutory or rule verification before filing?
